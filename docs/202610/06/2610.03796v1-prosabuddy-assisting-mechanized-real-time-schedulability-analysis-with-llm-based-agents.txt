Abstract —Rigorous schedulability analysis is essential for the design of hard real-time systems, yet errors in pen-and-paper proofs threaten the safety of critical applications. The Prosa initiative addresses this by offering a foundation for building machine-checkable schedulability analysis proofs in the Rocq proof assistant. However, the substantial time and expertise re-quired to construct such proofs remain a major barrier for wider adoption of Prosa. This work presents ProsaBuddy, an LLM-based agent system designed to lower the effort needed to develop mechanized real-time schedulability proofs. ProsaBuddy employs a ReAct loop with retrieval over the Prosa codebase, access to Rocq tools and optional human-written hints. It uses a subgoal-delegation architecture, decomposing a lemma into subgoals and dispatches them to subagents for proof. We evaluate ProsaBuddy on a mini benchmark drawn from real-time scheduling literature. Experiment results show that ProsaBuddy significantly outper-forms state-of-the-art LLM-based Rocq automated proving agent systems and a general coding agent OpenCode. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In hard real-time systems, rigorous real-time performance analysis is essential to guarantee the system’s timing behav-ior to consistently meet specified constraints. Researchers in this field typically develop schedulability analysis theorems that provide efficient methods for evaluating real-time perfor-mance. The development of such analyses has traditionally relied on human effort and has been conducted in a pen-and-paper manner. However, numerous recent reports have revealed errors in schedulability analyses documented in literatures [1]– [4]. Since real-time systems underpin safety-critical applica-tions, such analysis errors can lead to disastrous consequences. Prosa [5] is an initiative that aims to fundamentally ad-dress this problem. Prosa provides a repository of definitions and proofs for mechanized (i.e., machine-checkable) real-time schedulability analysis. It is built and verified using Rocq (formerly known as Coq) [6], a mature interactive theorem prover. Prosa builds dedicated infrastructure and methodolog-ical frameworks to enable detailed, reliable, yet readable real-time schedulability analysis. Widespread adoption of the Prosa approach within the real-time systems research community would significantly elevate the rigor, credibility, and founda-tional robustness of theoretical developments in the field. Despite its potential, a practical barrier prevents Prosa from achieving broader adoption: developing proofs in Rocq is very

Although Prosa has features that enhance its accessibility to real-time systems researchers, such as prioritiz-ing readability and adhering to established proof conventions within the community, the overhead of proof development remains substantial. According to our own experience, proving lemmas in a real-time scheduling paper in Rocq based on Prosa may require several weeks, even for those with moderate familiarity with Prosa and Rocq. For newcomers without prior experience, it is even more demanding. This substantial effort is likely a primary factor discouraging researchers from adopting the Prosa approach in their own work. In this work, we present ProsaBuddy, an LLM-based agent system designed to lower the effort barrier to adopting the Prosa approach for developing mechanized schedulability proofs. While LLMs have demonstrated impressive automated theorem proving capabilities in domains such as pure math-ematics, their application to theorem proving in real-time scheduling remains entirely unexplored. Although real-time scheduling lemmas can broadly be regarded as mathematical problems, they exhibit unique features that make them signifi-cantly more challenging to formalize and prove automatically than the pure-math problems in standard automated theorem proving benchmarks, as will be discussed in Section III-A. We build ProsaBuddy as an agent system that relies on inference-time scaling through an intensive ReAct (Reasoning & Acting) loop [7]. It combines retrieval over the Prosa codebase to supply necessary definitions and proof patterns, access to various Rocq tools which provide rich feedback in-formation, and optional human-written proof hints that provide high-level guidance. ProsaBuddy adopts a subgoal-delegation architecture: a primary agent decomposes the lemma into smaller subgoals and dispatches them to subagents, each receiving a structured task bundle that includes the subgoal, context, and a proof state snapshot. We enhance the agents with decomposition guidelines and domain-specific proving skills, improving their ability to navigate the deep proof chains. We conducted experiments to evaluate the performance of ProsaBuddy, comparing it with state-of-the-art LLM-based Rocq automated theorem proving systems, as well as ageneral-purpose coding agent OpenCode [8]. The comparison is conducted on a mini-benchmark of 24 lemmas that we built from the real-time scheduling literature; none of them appear in the existing Prosa library. Experimental results show that ProsaBuddy significantly outperforms all competing systems. arXiv:2610.03796v1 [cs.LO] 1 Oct 2026 We further extend the evaluation to a larger set of lemmas taken directly from the Prosa library, and the results confirm the superiority of ProsaBuddy. Ablation studies reveal that these gains arise from the combined effect of several design choices in ProsaBuddy. Additionally, we assess the impact of different backbone LLMs on the performance of ProsaBuddy. Overall, ProsaBuddy successfully proves non-trivial schedu-lability analysis lemmas, including some whose Rocq proofs require hundreds of tactics. Nevertheless, we do not currently position ProsaBuddy as a replacement for human researchers or as capable of proving such lemmas entirely independently. Instead, ProsaBuddy is designed to liberate researchers from the tedious low-level details of lengthy Rocq proofs, saving them time to focus on developing high-level proof ideas. However, given the rapid development of LLMs, a vast open space remains for exploring whether ProsaBuddy could, in the future, assist real-time scheduling research in more advanced ways, for instance, by independently proving open problems or even automatically developing new theories. II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

A. Rocq 

Rocq (formerly known as Coq) is a proof assistant based on dependent type theory, allowing users to write mathematical definitions, executable algorithms, and prove properties about them interactively. Following the project’s renaming, we will refer to Rocq exclusively when introducing ProsaBuddy. One key difference between human-written and Rocq proofs is the level of detail: a simple intuition often requires many explicit steps. B. Prosa 

Prosa [5] is a Rocq-based foundation for verified real-time scheduling theory. It emphasizes readable specifications, explicit assumptions, and proof reuse. Following the SS-Reflect methodology [10], Prosa encodes its specifications as computable boolean predicates, which effectively enables proof automation via reflection. This clarity also makes Prosa well-suited for LLM-based proof automation, as its good readability is informative not only to human users but also to LLM. Prosa has been extended to verified scheduling infrastructure in several directions in follow-up work, e.g., sustainability theory and weak sustainability [11], Abstract Response-Time Analysis (ARTA) based on the busy-window principle [12], FIFO response-time analysis with arbitrary deadlines and arrival curves [13], and connections between response-time analysis and network calculus [14]. Further-more, Prosa serves as the certificate generator for POET [15], the first foundational response-time analysis tool that produces fully machine-checkable Coq/Rocq certificates. Another re-cent work integrates Prosa with RefinedC [16], connecting Prosa’s verified response-time analysis with C implementa-tion verification, thereby enabling end-to-end certification of interrupt-free real-time schedulers implemented in low-level code. Together, these works make Prosa a useful infrastructure for verified real-time systems. Nevertheless, Prosa currently focuses on a core subset of real-time scheduling theory.

However, proofs in real-time scheduling often rely on recurring semantic structures, including scheduling assumptions, workload and processor models, invariants, response-time bounds, rewriting conventions, and sequences of lemmas. Existing methods treat reusable knowledge as retrieved premises, similar proofs, or short-term proof-state contexts. They do not make domain-specific proof procedures explicit as reusable skills with appli-cability conditions, required assumptions, expected effects and adaptation strategies. A. Overview 

Before presenting our approach, we first discuss the major differences between the problem tackled in this paper, auto-mated theorem proving for real-time schedulability analysis, and the more commonly studied application of LLM-based automated theorem proving to pure mathematics. The theorem-proving capability of LLMs is obtained via pre-training on large-scale corpora such as Mathlib [60]. In contrast, public data for mechanized schedulability analysis is very limited. The Prosa library is the main available resource, but its volume is orders of magnitude smaller than the cor-pora for mathematics. This scarcity fundamentally constrains the ability of LLMs to learn proof patterns specific to the schedulability analysis domain. The schedulability analysis lemmas are substantially more complex than those found in mathematics benchmarks. They are built upon a complex hierarchy of concepts, requiring many intricate definitions that often recursively depend on one another across multiple levels, along with numerous hypothe-ses. For example, in the PutnamBench suite [61] for evaluating LLM-based mathematics theorem proving, the description of a lemma spans no more than 20 lines. In contrast, the complete description of a schedulability analysis lemma in Prosa, including all definitions, variables, assumptions, and the lemma itself, often extends to several hundred lines. This increased length and conceptual depth impose a significantly heavier burden on LLM’s context handling and reasoning capabilities. Beyond the complexity of individual lemmas, Prosa has a highly structured architecture that promotes lemma reuse across different task models. Whether an existing lemma can be applied to prove a target lemma depends on whether its preconditions hold, and those preconditions, in turn, rely on further preconditions, ultimately tracing back to the specific assumptions of the task model under consideration. This unique feature has introduced extra difficulty in the proving. We will further elaborate on this point in Section III-C. To address the above challenges, ProsaBuddy adopts several complementary strategies. First, because pre-training cannot supply sufficient domain knowledge, we rely on inference-time scaling: the LLM repeatedly attempts the proof, using feedback from external tools. Second, we leverage the existing Prosa library to retrieve relevant definitions and lemmas, and to learn proving techniques by imitating how Prosa handles similar problems. This demands a powerful retrieval capability capable of accurately locating useful information within Prosa. Third, we incorporate optional human-provided proof hints, which help the agent navigate deep proof chains that would otherwise be difficult to resolve automatically. For these considerations, we build ProsaBuddy on top of OpenCode [8] rather than constructing an agent system from scratch. OpenCode provides a solid foundation that satisfies the requirements outlined above. In OpenCode, an agent follows an iterative ReAct (Reasoning & Acting) loop [7]: it queries the LLM, uses the reasoning in the model’s output to execute external tools, and then appends the feedback to the conversation context for the next query, repeating this process until the task is resolved. OpenCode offers powerful code search capabilities well-suited for retrieval over the Prosa library and allows users to inject human-written proof hints into the agent’s prompt easily. By building on OpenCode, we inherit a mature agent foundation and can focus our efforts on developing high-level capabilities tailored to the needs of schedulability analysis lemma proving. Figure 2 shows the major components of ProsaBuddy. The system takes as input a lemma formalized in Rocq, along with 

optional human-written proof hints. The output is a verified proof in Rocq, or a failure declaration. For proof construction, ProsaBuddy uses LLM-based agents in a subgoal-delegation architecture. The primary agent decomposes the lemma into smaller, independent subgoals and dispatches them to special-ized subagents. The subagent leverages OpenCode’s existing infrastructure, which already supports launching subagents. When launching a subagent, the system does not simply forward the original prompt. Instead, it builds a structured task bundle that includes the subgoal to prove, the necessary context, and a snapshot of the current proof state, for the subagent to solve the assigned subgoal independently. We detail the lemma decomposition process in Section III-B. The agents retrieve relevant information from the Prosa codebase using OpenCode’s standard file-reading and code-search tools, such as file browsing and grep-like search. Beyond these standard capabilities, we enable ProsaBuddy to invoke Rocq tools directly to get rich feedback information for its ReAct loop. We elaborate on these external tools in Section III-D. We also equip the agents with a collection of proving skills that target common challenges in the proving procedure, which will be elaborated in Section III-C. Last but not least, the proof submitted by the agents must go through auditing before it is finally accepted as a valid proof of the target lemma, which will be introduced in Section III-E.

C. Proving Skills 

We develop proving skills to enhance the subagent’s ability to prove delegated subgoals. These skills are generated by us-ing the LLM to analyze the proving traces, using the following process. First, we select 65 lemmas from Prosa (only a small fraction of Prosa) as training lemmas 1. The selection criteria aim for diversity: we choose lemmas with proofs that vary in tactic count (i.e., difficulty), and we sample from different files and, whenever possible, different subdirectories within the Prosa codebase. We use a preliminary version of our system (equipped with external Rocq tools and the Prosa codebase) to attempt to prove these lemmas, and we collect the full traces of the proving procedure. Each trace captures the agent’s iterative attempts, including failures, error messages, and proof states, until success. We then fed these traces to an LLM to analyze failure reasons and generate skills. This insight becomes a reusable proving skill. Why does analyzing proof traces enable the LLM to gen-erate useful skills? The traces contain rich diagnostic infor-mation: they show iterative attempts to solve a local problem, with clear success/failure outcomes, error messages, and the current proof state at each step. The LLM can observe how the agent fails repeatedly and how it eventually succeeds. The resulting proving skill thus encodes a practical solution that the LLM alone would not produce. It is worth comparing proving skills with the decomposi-tion guidelines. Both serve the same purpose: adding extra information to the LLM’s prompt to improve its problem-solving ability. The differences lie in how they are obtained and used. Decomposition guidelines are crafted manually by human experts, while proving skills are generated by having the LLM analyze proof traces, followed by human review. We also attempted to use a similar trace-driven process to extract decomposition guidelines, but the results were poor. This is probably because the impact of a decomposition is non-local and diffuse; the LLM struggles to connect later proof failures to decomposition choices and infer how the decomposition should be refined. Moreover, while proving skills are stored in skill files and loaded on demand, decomposition guidelines are placed directly in the agent’s prompt. The current set of decomposition guidelines is small, so the benefit of making them load-on-demand is limited, but nothing prevents users from packaging them as on-demand skill files. 

D. Tools 

In addition to the standard built-in tools in OpenCode, such as file reading and code search, we develop several extra tools to assist the proof construction. We integrate a suite of Rocq tools that supply fine-grained, context-aware feedback based on the current proof state. Rocq provides a batch compiler and an interactive toplevel, i.e., a REPL (read–eval–print loop). rocq-lsp is a separate lan-guage server/plugin for Rocq. Our tools wrap these underlying interfaces. We develop a compiler-backed tool that checks whole .v files and reports compilation status and errors, a toplevel-backed tool that answers queries about library objects, lemma types, assumptions, and definitions, and a 

rocq-lsp -backed tool that provides incremental diagnostics, current goals and local hypotheses. Our integration follows OpenCode’s standard way of providing tools. Each tool is a module with a call name, a brief description, and an input schema. The agent does not run Rocq directly. It selects a tool and supplies arguments, and the OpenCode runtime dispatches the call, using session configuration such as file paths and project roots. The output is written to files, which the OpenCode harness parses into structured results such as error locations, goals, and hypotheses. This gives the agent fine-grained proof-state feedback while avoiding fragile raw command-line handling. We developed a structured proof-planning tool that enables the primary agent to externalize its decomposition as an explicit DAG before constructing a proof sketch that can be compiled by Rocq. Each node in the DAG records a Rocq subgoal, its dependencies, and optionally proposed library lemmas; terminal delegation candidates are materialized as annotated proof regions. The tool mechanically checks target-lemma consistency, acyclicity, and lemma applicability, and compiles the resulting sketch to ensure the subgoals can be combined. It also assigns each delegated subgoal a unique proof region, so a subagent can edit only its assigned region while preserving the surrounding proof and local context. This tool is useful since, if a decomposition remains only in the primary agent’s free-form reasoning, invalid decompositions may go unnoticed until subagents fail. We also developed a tool to detect stagnation in proof construction. Since the Rocq compiler reports only the cur-rent proof state, it cannot determine whether repeated agent actions are advancing the proof or reproducing the same failure; without an independent progress assessment, the agent may keep calling the same tools, launching subagents on unchanged proof regions, or making superficial edits while wasting computation and tokens. E. Proof Auditing 

When an agent declares a proof complete, we cannot treat that declaration, or even a successful compilation, as evidence that the proof has actually been written, since the agent may “cheat” during proof construction. For example, an agent might silently change the theorem statement, weaken a definition, or introduce an unproven axiom. This could still result in a successful compilation, yet not give a valid proof of the intended lemma. We therefore need an independent auditing step to decide whether the proof is valid. The goal of proof auditing is to ensure two things: (1) the lemma to be proved is unchanged, and (2) the generated proof indeed proves the lemma rather than bypassing its proof. ProsaBuddy runs in a sandbox, an isolated workspace that limits which files the agent can read and write. We place the libraries the agent is allowed to use in its read-only scope, so that the agent can import from them but cannot modify or recompile the libraries themselves. Based on this, the auditing consists of the following steps. First, we check whether the submitted .v file, which con-tains the lemma to be proved and its proof script, is accepted by Rocq. Concretely, this means that when the Rocq compiler processes the file, it is parsed and elaborated without errors, and the proof terms produced by the tactics are accepted by the Rocq kernel. In particular, if the proof script contains tactics that leave unfinished goals, such as admit or give_up , the check fails at this step. Second, we check that the proof body, i.e., the part after 

Proof. and before Qed. , contains only commands for advancing or querying the proof state. Table IV shows the classification of Rocq commands. Among these classes, only the VtProofStep and VtQuery classes are allowed in the proof body; all other classes are forbidden. We use the 

astdump plugin (part of rocq-lsp ) to extract the AST of the .v file and check whether there is an AST node corresponding to a command in a forbidden class within the proof body. If so, the check fails at this step. Third, we verify that nothing outside the proof body has been changed, except for imports from trusted libraries at the very beginning. Specifically, before the agent starts working, we store a copy of the original .v file containing the target lemma. After the agent submits its .v file, we compare the ASTs of the two files to ensure that any changes outside the proof body are limited to imports from trusted libraries at the very beginning; otherwise, the check fails. Currently, we treat only MathComp and Prosa as trusted libraries and assume that importing them at the beginning of the .v file does not introduce risks of altering the lemma to be proved or enabling the agent to bypass the proof. A proof submitted by the agent is deemed valid only if it passes all the steps above. Otherwise, the submitted proof is rejected and returned to the agent for reconstruction. Note that the audit is performed in a different OS process, so any changes to the Rocq environment state made by the proving agents cannot affect the Rocq environment state used in the audit. The above auditing procedure is conservative in the sense that a proof file submitted by the agent is not necessarily invalid just because it failed the audit. Nevertheless, the sound-ness of our proof auditing, both its design and implementation, has not yet been formally verified. Establishing a formally verified proof auditing procedure will be our future work. Besides our proof-auditing script itself, we rely on several trusted bases to declare a proof valid. The scope of the trusted base depends on the property being checked. For kernel-level proof validity, the trusted base is the Rocq kernel: it is the component that actually checks proof terms, and Rocq is designed so that nothing else needs to be trusted for soundness. ProsaBuddy’s proof-auditing procedure, however, relies on additional components. Our assumed trusted base for auditing consists of the Rocq kernel, the Rocq compiler, the tactics allowed to be used, the trusted libraries, the astdump plugin, and our proof-auditing script. Trust to these components is needed because our audit does not inspect the proof term directly. It inspects the AST and checks whether the AST contains forbidden commands. The absence of such commands in the AST, however, does not by itself guarantee that the proof term submitted to the kernel is free of them. The AST is extracted from the source file by the astdump plugin, and the proof term is obtained by compiling the source file, during which the tactics are executed. To infer from the absence of forbidden commands in the AST that the proof term is also free of them, we have to trust the astdump plugin, the compiler, the tactics and the imported libraries, as well as our proof-auditing script. A bug in any of these components might cause our auditing procedure to overlook an invalid proof, even though the Rocq kernel itself remains sound. However, the tools that agents use while generating proofs need not be trusted: they are used only to provide information guiding agents to construct proofs, and the correctness of this information does not affect the auditing procedure. IV. E VALUATION 

A. Comparison with State-of-the-Art 

We compare ProsaBuddy with four recent LLM-based au-tomated theorem proving systems for Coq/Rocq: PALM [33], AutoRocq [41], Cobblestone [40] and RocqStar [39]. The interaction layer with Coq of PALM and Cobblestone relies on coq-serapi, which has only been maintained up to Coq 8.20. This dependency prevents PALM from being directly applied to the Rocq environment. Since our experiments use Rocq 9.0 for RocqStar and ProsaBuddy, we ported the subset of coq-serapi interaction functionality used by PALM to Rocq 9.0 for a fair comparison. We also attempted to evaluate COPRA [34], another recent LLM-based Coq/Rocq proving agent, but COPRA failed on all benchmark tasks in our environment; therefore, we do not include its results in Table VI. We investigated the failures and found that, although COPRA claims support for both Lean and Coq, the released GitHub implementation leaves several Coq-environment con-nection functions unimplemented. We did not compare with Rango [37] because RocqStar has already included a direct comparison with Rango and reported better performance [39]. Therefore, we only choose the better one as the representative baseline in our experiments. We did not compare with CoqPi-lot because RocqStar is a follow-up system that extends the CoqPilot pipeline with a more advanced agentic architecture [32], [39]. We did not compare with ADAPT [38], QUARRY [43], ReCent-Prover [42] and the work in [35] and [36] because they are not yet publicly available. Moreover, we also include the original OpenCode in the comparison. For all systems, we use GPT-5.4 as the backbone LLM. All compared systems have access to the full Prosa library. The evaluation is conducted on a benchmark that we cre-ated. The benchmark contains 24 lemma formalizations in Rocq, which are not included in the existing Prosa library. These lemmas are drawn from five research papers [63]–[66], [68] and one book [67] in real-time scheduling theory, and we manually formalized them in Rocq. This ensures that the benchmark has no overlap with the “training” lemmas used to automatically generate the proving skills, as introduced in Sec-tion III-C. The sources of these lemmas are listed in Table V. All lemmas concern global multiprocessor scheduling. Note that, in Table V, case #10 and #11 (and also #12 and #13) come from manually splitting a single lemma into separate smaller lemmas. We do this when the original lemma states multiple independent results at once. This does not change the mathematical content of the original result. There is a compatibility issue: Prosa 0.4 includes foundations for global multiprocessor scheduling, but the latest version, Prosa 0.6, does not have this part. Prosa 0.6 is based on Rocq, whereas Prosa 0.4 is based on Coq, and Rocq and Coq have subtle syntactic differences. Therefore, we manually ported the global multiprocessor scheduling part in Prosa 0.4 from Coq to Rocq. We use the relevant portion of the corresponding research paper as the human-written proof hint input. Specifically, this input includes the target theorem or lemma itself, its full human-written proof, as well as any supporting definitions, no-tation, and variables that are referenced within the proof. Au-toRocq, OpenCode and ProsaBuddy accept this input through their existing interfaces. However, PALM, Cobblestone, and RocqStar do not originally provide such an interface. PALM and Cobblestone operate through their generate-and-repair workflows, which take the formal lemma and the current proof state as inputs and iteratively attempt to construct a valid proof. Neither PALM nor Cobblestone accepts additional text inputs. RocqStar retrieves formal definitions and theorems from a structured Rocq library, but it does not accept free-text descriptions as input. To provide the same human-written proof hint to all systems, we augmented PALM, Cobblestone, and RocqStar to prepend the corresponding hint to their system prompts. All comparison results reported for PALM, Cobblestone, and RocqStar are obtained from their augmented version with human-written hints. Table VI shows the comparison results. B. Ablation Study 

We conduct ablation experiments to evaluate the contri-bution of different components in ProsaBuddy, the result of which is also reported in Table VI. 

PB-w/o-S . This variant removes the proving skills from ProsaBuddy. It proves 12 lemmas, fewer than the full Pros-aBuddy does, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proving skills developed in Section III-C. 

PB-w/o-D . This variant removes the lemma decomposition mechanism and instead uses only the primary agent to prove a lemma. PB-w/o-D proves 14 lemmas. The gap between PB-w/o-D and ProsaBuddy mainly appears on harder cases, i.e., lemmas on which ProsaBuddy spends more tokens. Token counts reveal a trade-off: ProsaBuddy sometimes spends more tokens because planning and delegation introduce extra inter-action cost, but this overhead enables proofs that the single-agent variant cannot complete. 

PB-w/o-H . This variant removes the human-written proof hints from ProsaBuddy, i.e., it does not read the corresponding paper content. PB-w/o-H proves 15 lemmas. The two cases where ProsaBuddy succeeds but PB-w/o-H does not are both relatively difficult lemmas. This suggests that for simple cases, the formal context in Prosa, including definitions, existing lemmas, and proof states, already provides substantial guid-ance for proof construction. However, for difficult lemmas, the human-written proof hints play a significant role. 

PB-w/o-P . This variant removes access to the full Prosa library and only provides the minimal dependent files needed to state the target lemmas. As a result, it can use basic defi-nitions and low-level lemmas (related to the basic properties of the definitions) included in the dependent files, but cannot directly observe or reuse higher-level auxiliary lemmas in Prosa. PB-w/o-P proves 12 lemmas. This shows that a full library context is important for extending an existing mecha-nized development. Without it, the agent needs to rediscover intermediate facts that are already available in Prosa, which signiciantly increase the difficulty to complete the proof. C. Different Backbone LLMs 

We further evaluate how the choice of different backbone LLM affects the performance of ProsaBuddy. The results are reported in Table IX. In these experiments, all components of ProsaBuddy are held constant except the backbone LLM. From the results we can see that the backbone LLM has a substantial impact on proof success: with the most powerful model, GPT-5.4, ProsaBuddy proves 17 lemmas, compared to 9 with DeepSeek-v4-flash, 6 with DeepSeek-v4-pro, 2 with Gemini-3.1-flash-lite, and 1 with GPT-oss-120B. Unexpect-edly, DeepSeek-v4-flash outperforms DeepSeek-v4-pro. We currently do not have a clear explanation for this anomaly and will investigate its cause in future work. Overall, these results show that the agent framework, augmented external knowledge, and feedback from Rocq tools are not sufficient on their own; the reasoning capability of the backbone LLM is crucial for the performance of ProsaBuddy. We also observe that, on the subset of lemmas the weaker models can prove, they tend to consume more tokens on average than GPT-5.4, with a few exceptions. Similar to above, the mark “–” in Table IX denotes the omitted experiments to save time and cost, as for the difficult cases GPT-5.4 failed, the other weaker LLMs likely will also fail. V. D ISCUSSIONS AND CONCLUSIONS 

In this work, we present ProsaBuddy, an LLM-based agent system designed to lower the effort needed to develop mech-anized real-time schedulability proofs. Evaluations show that ProsaBuddy can successfully prove many non-trivial lemmas. However, this is only a preliminary attempt in this direction; it has many limitations and ample room for further exploration. Although in this paper we introduce and evaluate Pros-aBuddy as a standalone system that proves lemmas end-to-end, our ultimate goal is to build ProsaBuddy as a highly interactive tool. When a human expert intervenes in the proving process and provides guidance at points where ProsaBuddy gets stuck, ProsaBuddy becomes more effective and useful. We did not systematically evaluate ProsaBuddy with a human in the loop, because designing an objective and reproducible evaluation methodology for such interaction would be difficult. Never-theless, we conducted some ad-hoc interactive experiments. For example, for cases # 13 and # 14 in Table VI, which ProsaBuddy could not prove alone, a researcher with exper-tise in real-time scheduling theory worked interactively with ProsaBuddy, injecting additional human instructions when ProsaBuddy was stuck or actively requested support. In this way, both lemmas were successfully proved in 2 hours and 3

hours, respectively. For comparison, a second researcher with a similar background independently proved them fully manually in Rocq, requiring 14 hours and 17 hours, respectively. A critical issue in automated theorem proving is the formal-ization of the lemmas themselves. This is especially important for schedulability lemmas, whose descriptions are lengthy and involve many definitions and hypotheses. Correct formaliza-tion is the foundation for the proof work: if a lemma is incorrectly formalized, its proof is meaningless. Therefore, the lemma must be formalized very carefully, a task that itself demands considerable effort. Assisting researchers during this formalization phase is thus also valuable. However, while lemma proving benefits from an automatic verifier (Rocq) that can check correctness, formalization lacks such a verifier. It is impossible to fully automate the checking of whether a formal-ization precisely captures the human’s intended meaning. Our future work will investigate how to utilize an LLM-based agent to assist the formalization of schedulability analysis lemmas. The decomposition guidelines and proving skills introduced in Section III are only a small subset of what can be added to improve the agent’s capabilities. We expect that ProsaBuddy users will develop their own skills to enhance the agent, and we look forward to an ecosystem in which users share their skills to collectively improve the system. Such an ecosys-tem would also create a feedback loop in which successful skills are refined, reused, and benchmarked across projects, accelerating collective improvement. On the other hand, these guidelines and skills are tailored to the capabilities of today’s LLMs. Given how rapidly LLMs are advancing, future LLMs may have much stronger reasoning abilities, rendering many previously useful skills obsolete. ProsaBuddy has the potential to be used more broadly. Proofreader [69] is an agentic system that helps researchers and authors find flaws in their own proofs. It actively seeks counterexamples and errors, without aiming to establish formal correctness. Given these complementary strengths, future work could explore how ProsaBuddy and other tools like Proof-reader might jointly accelerate the discovery, refinement, and validation of new theorems.
